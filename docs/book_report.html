Introduction

Over the course of this semester, I read the self-help book Deep Work by Cal Newport. The principles and strategies discussed in the book were illuminating, especially in my personal and work habits. The term “deep work” is defined by Newport as: “Professional activities performed in a state of distraction-free concentration that push your cognitive capabilities to their limit. These efforts create new value, improve your skill, and are hard to replicate” (Newport 3). This skill, Newport claims, is becoming increasingly rare in today’s society. A person’s ability to thrive in this economy will be heavily dependent on cultivating deep work. In a typical workday, many knowledge workers are burdened with minor tasks or choose to engage in distractions that hardly contribute to their primary role. For example, scanning emails, setting up meetings, and scrolling through social media are all considered “distractions”. Newport categorizes these activities under “shallow work,” which is defined as “Noncognitively demanding, logistical-style tasks, often performed while distracted. These efforts tend to not create much new value in the world and are easy to replicate” (Newport 6). Due to these steady interruptions, many people’s “concentration muscles” are weak, resulting in slow productivity and even a lack of meaning in their lives. To solve this epidemic, Newport cites the habits and productivity rate of successful workers to convince his readers the benefits of developing the ability to work deeply. These habits are generalized into four rules to help us rewire our brain to dispose of shallow work habits and train our ability to concentrate. I will share three specific habits that I have implemented since reading this book.

Habit 1: Make Your Place of Work Distraction-Free

While developing his psychological theses, Carl Jung would retreat to a private cabin and lock himself in a room to write. The time other than writing would be spent walking around the woods, pondering and formulating more thoughts to write. With these habits, Carl Jung was able to pioneer a groundbreaking branch of psychology that shapes how we approach mental health, personality theory, and cultural studies today. Newport shares this story to illustrate the power of working without distractions. As someone who is easily distracted by my phone, the first change I made to my workplace is setting my phone to a place where I can’t see it. By doing so, I’ve found it’s been much easier to work longer and enter into the flow of deep work. Another detail I’ve changed is to clear the clutter around the desk I work at. Cleaning out that specific area has translated into cleaning out the clutter in my brain. Consequently, my brain can switch to the “working” gear and concentrate far more deeply.

During my internship this past summer, there were times where I was distracted by my phone during slower weeks. I couldn’t seem to focus for very long on projects I was working on as my mind would drift to the dings or buzzes from my phone. I would go home feeling unaccomplished and a lack of fulfillment in those days. Although I finished the projects as requested, it definitely could have been more professional and polished. I came to the realization that if I wanted to reflect responsible professional practices, I needed to concentrate on my work. The first and most effective change was blocking all social media apps during the workday. Without all the buzzing to draw me away from my work, I finished projects quicker, better, and learned other crucial skills with the extra time I had. As I approach graduation and start my full-time career post-college, I want to continue this habit at my new workplace. I truly believe that it can help me work more efficiently, produce higher-quality results, and find fulfillment in my career.

Habit 2: Plan Out Your Day

According to a study that observed people’s perception of how long they worked, “people who claimed to work sixty to sixty-four hours per week were actually averaging more like forty-four hours per week, while those claiming to work more than seventy-five hours were actually working less than fifty-five” (Newport 222). Newport uses this study to highlight that people don’t really pay attention to their time, and therefore, allow small distractions to accumulate into their schedule. These trivial matters detract from being able to maximize our productivity each day. He proposes a seemingly extreme solution: plan each minute of your day. At first, I thought this was overkill. Who’s got the time to do that? I then realized that I’ve already planned most of my weekdays. My mornings and afternoons already consist of school and work. All I really have to do is schedule my evenings and every minute would be scheduled. I decided to try out this habit for a week. During this experiment, my immediate homework and work due at the end of the week were finished on Wednesday, and I was able to get started on other homework ahead of schedule. As a skeptic, I was amazed that a minor tweak could have such a large impact. I encourage those who frequently encounter idle moments in their day to try this out.

Habit 3: Fast From Social Media

Since the book’s publication in 2016, the influence of social media in our lives has only grown exponentially. Newport argues that any engagement in social media apps saps your ability to perform deep work. “They’re not really all that important in your life,” (Newport 206) he summarizes succinctly. However, I believe that working in social media can be a form of deep work. Planning, writing, filming, editing, and posting can require large amounts of concentration and dedication. Of course, there is a spectrum of engagement in social media that we must consider. For the vast majority of people, the extent of their activities is scrolling through pictures from friends or watching short-form videos. Only a very small percentage are engaged in actively posting content to earn income. As someone who only uses social media for entertainment, I wholeheartedly agree with Newport’s observations on its addictive nature and detriment to our concentration ability.

After sharing these thoughts with my wife, we’ve decided to fast from all social media apps for a week. I’ve already seen immediate benefits in my everyday life. For example, it’s taken me around half the time to finish all my necessary homework and other tasks. The fast has also brought to light other meaningful activities I could do instead of scrolling. I’ve cleaned and organized the house more, reduced the time it takes to get ready in the morning, and read further into my books. If I had to pick only one habit to incorporate out of all the ones mentioned in the book, it would be this one. Your mind and body will thank you for it.

Conclusion

Deep work has transformed the way I approach school and work. Many prior tasks would take many hours or even days when all I needed was to concentrate for an hour or two to finish. After reading through this book and applying some basic principles, it’s been much easier to quiet the voice in my head to seek distractions. What used to be stressful Fridays to finish lingering assignments have turned into quality time with friends and family. If you want to stop wasting time and start unlocking your brain’s untapped potential to achieve, give this book a read.
